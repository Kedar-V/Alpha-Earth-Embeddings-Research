# Quantitative scales and axis limits

Axis limits change the story. Choose them on purpose. When you are asked, say which of the three approaches you used.

## Bars start at zero

Bar length is read as magnitude. A bar axis that does not include zero exaggerates differences.

Course example: a candidate's vote share looked about twice the prior candidates' because the axis started near 14%. Labeled values were about 14%, 16%, 15%, and 17%. An axis from zero shows they are close. Adding earlier years (a 26% result) changed the narrative again. Do not cherry-pick the window. Extra honest context often reverses the claim.

Same failure, smaller: a bar chart of a rate from 35 to 40 makes a move from 35 to 39.6 look like a collapse. Start at zero.

## Other charts: zero is a choice

Lines, dots, and slopes are read as position and angle, not as length from a baseline. Forcing zero can hide a small change that matters. Dropping zero can also exaggerate a wiggle.

Course example: a time series of body temperature or a similar tightly bounded measure. With zero on the axis, the real difference disappears. Zoomed to the data, the same difference can look enormous. Neither picture is automatically honest. Match the claim, and do not invent a crisis or hide one.

## Three ways to set limits

1. **Realistic range.** Values that can occur in this context. An annual U.S. temperature chart from 0°F to 100°F includes weather these data never approach, and a chart that runs far below any temperature of the last several hundred million years is a rhetorical trick, not context.
2. **Range of the data.** Use when the story is the observed variation. Say so if a truncated view could be misread as a large relative change.
3. **Both.** Full scale, plus a callout of the data range, when the audience needs the overview and the detail.

Often the better fix is a different metric, not a stretched axis. Annual temperature compared with an 1875–1900 baseline is a small wiggle on a 0–100°F scale and an obvious rise as a temperature anomaly. If you know the message, plot the quantity that carries it.

Do not drop earlier periods to manufacture a trend. If a longer series changes the conclusion, show the longer series or state the window in the title.

## Dual axes

Dual y-axes are easy to misread: zeros at different heights, arbitrary scaling, a correlation the axes created.

The same two series can be drawn to look tightly coupled, unrelated, or inversely related by changing only the scales. A shared chart with mismatched zero lines is misleading even when each series, alone, did not need a zero baseline.

Do not use a dual axis to "show the relationship." Use one of these:

- **Side-by-side panels** with the same time axis and comparable design. Two series with very different magnitudes (one near 2, one near 80) each get a readable scale without implying they share a unit.
- **An index chart** when the claim is relative change. Set both series to 0% (or 100) at the same start date and plot percent change. Example shape: German GDP up 28% since 2004 versus global GDP up 76%. The index shows the comparison; a footnote can hold the absolute levels.
- **Two figures** when the series measure different things.

A dual axis that pairs a rate with its own count is still two different encodings. Prefer the panel or the index unless the user has a reason a stakeholder chart already uses and you label both axes in words, with matching baselines.
