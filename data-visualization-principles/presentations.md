# Presentations

The goal is to change the audience: they learned something, they think differently, or they want to act. Design the talk for that change, not as a record of the project.

A good presentation:

- Tells one focused story, obvious and short
- Uses little text, and only visuals that serve the story
- Follows basic graphic design (proximity, alignment, repetition, contrast)
- Points the audience at one thing at a time

## Cut to the trip you are taking

You are taking the work on the road. The full sequence will not fit: literature review, methods, collection, cleaning, more collection, specification, sensitivity, adjustment, interpretation.

Write two lists before you cut:

- **Objective.** What should they know, think, or do when you stop?
- **Audience.** What do they know, think, and do today?

Keep essential background, essential methods, essential analysis, essential findings, essential recommendations. "Essential" means it changes the objective for this audience. Everything else is backup or it is deleted.

## ABT

Frame the talk as And / But / Therefore (Randy Olson, *The Narrative Gym*).

- **And** is shared background. Agreement. Context they already accept.
- **But** is the conflict. The reason the background is not enough.
- **Therefore** is what you did, found, or need them to do.

Example: less than half of students graduate on time, and NCAN students graduate at a still lower rate, but some in-college programs beat pre-college programs, therefore we tested which services those programs provide.

A chain of Ands is a list of background. It does not move. Put a But / Therefore turn on each section, not one ABT on the title slide and then a chronology.

How to find the cuts:

1. Tell the story out loud to yourself or to a friend. Record it.
2. Use the transcript to mark sections and delete anything that is not essential.
3. Check the slides or the poster against that spoken story. If a slide is not in the story, it does not go in the main talk.

Storyboard before you design slides. Post-its or a digital board: one idea and its visual per card. Add the chart or the diagram after the sequence is right.

## Slide checklist

- Limit text. Replace it with a diagram or a chart.
- Apply proximity, alignment, repetition, and contrast.
- Active titles, same standard as a figure: the finding, not the topic.
- Remove anything that does not serve the message.
- Guide attention. One beat at a time.

## Text

People do not read a paragraph and listen to you at the same time. If the slide is a block of prose, they are not hearing the sentence you are speaking.

- Only crucial details.
- Short phrases, not sentences they must finish while you talk.
- Replace process prose with a diagram. Diagrams carry comparisons, frameworks, hierarchies, processes, systems, and relationships.
- Replace a result paragraph with a chart whose title is the result. "Hires always peak in July, the start of the fiscal year" plus the monthly series beats a bullet that says the same thing.

Sketch two or three diagram layouts on paper before you open a tool. Software makes a first idea feel final. Illustrator helps when you need curved connectors, select-similar, alignment, and layers. PowerPoint shapes, lines, and text are enough for most diagrams.

## PARC

From Robin Williams, *The Non-Designer's Design Book*. Four checks on every slide.

### Proximity

Group related items by putting them close. Separate unrelated items with whitespace. The brain groups what sits together.

Too little space merges three ideas into one clump. Too much space splits one idea into stray lines. After you group, the viewer should be able to say "three blocks" without reading the words.

A heading that sits closer to the previous bullet than to its own bullets is in the wrong group. Move it.

### Alignment

Nothing is placed arbitrarily. Every edge lines up with another edge.

- Left-align text, especially when it wraps. Centered lines start in a different place each row, so the eye has to search.
- Right-align numbers so the digits line up.
- One alignment axis on a slide is stronger than a title in the center, a chart on the left, and a caption floating in between.

### Repetition

Repeat font, size, weight, color, and alignment so the deck feels like one piece. In a table of related models (bystander CPR, AED use, survival), the row structure, the reference row, and the header treatment should repeat. Repetition is what makes a difference in weight or color readable as a signal instead of as clutter.

### Contrast

Contrast decides what is seen first: size, weight, and color. The brain registers a strong difference immediately. Use it for the claim.

- The title is larger and heavier than the body.
- The focal series is the saturated color; comparison series are grey (Honda versus other makers, once the claim is about Honda).
- A wall of equal bullets has no contrast. Promote the three real points to headings and demote the rest, or cut them.

If everything is bold, nothing is.

## Remove furniture

Templates add borders, corner logos, colored bars, and lines that are not data. The eye often lands on that corner first, and there is no information there. Mystery rules ("what do the blue lines mean?") cost attention.

- Prefer a white background. A colored background restricts text to white or to text boxes, and photographs look like stickers.
- White keeps contrast flexible, lets images sit without a knocked-out background, and lets slides from different decks mix.
- Delete the border. It is not a frame the content needs.

## Guide attention

Reveal elements as you discuss them, so the room is looking at the same thing. If someone looks away, the newest mark tells them where you are.

Build the result in beats, each beat a slide or a simple appear:

1. The three metrics, named and nothing else.
2. Most colleges improved.
3. Some declined, especially on retention.
4. A handful met the goal.
5. These three were selected for the case study.

You will have more slides and you will move through them faster. That is the point. One slide with every annotation already on it invites the audience to read ahead and miss the argument.

Do not use the build to hide a caveat the audience needs before the claim. The caveat is its own beat, before the recommendation.
